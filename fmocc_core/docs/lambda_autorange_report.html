Finding the λ range automatically

A procedure that measures each method's λ response curve for a given reward and prompt pair, brackets its transition, and places the sweep grid where the energy/reward frontier actually moves — so a large sweep can be left running without hand-tuning λ per reward.

00Summary

01Why a fixed λ grid wastes most of its points

λ is not one knob. In MPC it is a Lagrange weight the optimiser solves against (J = E/(2λ) − r); in HardFlow it enters as the deviation penalty reg = N/(2λ), which is the same physical quantity per step but is also bounded below by a solver-stability floor λ ≥ N·lr/2; in FMRG it is an open-loop gain on the reward gradient, z ← z + (dt/n_opt)·λ·∇r, with nothing minimising energy at all. On top of that, its useful range moves with the reward's scale: the repo's own note is that switching LPIPS → ImageReward de-regularises by ~200× at fixed λ.

The consequence for an unattended sweep is that a grid chosen for one (method, reward) lands in the wrong regime for another. The suite grid {0.1, 1, 10, 50, 200, 1000, 3000, 10000} was built for MPC on ImageReward — and even there, MPC is 79% of the way to the reward ceiling at λ=1 and fully saturated by λ=10, so six of the eight points measure the same plateau. FMRG on CLIP needs λ three to four orders of magnitude higher than MPC on ImageReward to move at all.

The thing that is invariant is the shape of the response. For every method and every reward, Δr(λ) = r_ctrl − r_unc is ≈0 while the effort penalty dominates, rises through a transition, and plateaus at whatever the reward model can be pushed to. A grid is only worth its cost where that curve is moving. So the automation problem is: find the transition for this (method, reward, prompt) cheaply, and put the points there.

02The procedure

Implemented as fmocc_core/lambda_autorange.py. It runs in one process — FLUX loads once, the reward model loads once per (reward, edit prompt) — and calls run_sweep._run_one directly, so every guard in that path (locked defaults, plausibility ranges, the non-finite checks) still applies. Range-finding uses a cheap proxy configuration (N=4; MPC with the jump surrogate and n_inner=6; HardFlow at the authors' n_inner=40, lr=2.5; FMRG at n_opt=3) and emits a grid for the N you will actually sweep.

  1. Probes (seconds, once per reward). One uncontrolled rollout gives r_unc. The reward's attainable maximum comes from reward_ranges.REWARD_OBSERVED_RANGE, so Δr_max ≈ r_max − r_unc is the ceiling the curve should plateau at. One backward pass through the decoder and the reward model at the uncontrolled endpoint gives ‖∇_z r‖. Linearising the reward around that endpoint, the λ at which the whole attainable range gets bought scales as Δr_max / ‖∇_z r‖² for all three methods (MPC and HardFlow through the energy trade-off, FMRG because its total displacement is ≈ λ‖∇r‖ over the trajectory). That gives a scale-aware starting λ₀; the constant in front was set once from the ImageReward study and only has to be right to within a decade or two, because the next step corrects it.
  2. Bracket. Evaluate λ₀, then step ×10 upward until the top is flat — the last decade bought less than tol (5% of the ceiling) while already high, or Δr is within 10% of the ceiling, or energy tripled for no reward — and ×10 downward until the bottom is inactive (Δr ≤ tol). Runs that raise, such as HardFlow below its lr·reg ≥ 1 floor, are recorded as the admissible boundary rather than retried. Budget: 10 evaluations per method.
  3. Refine. Fit a logistic in log₁₀λ to the measured points (numpy grid search, no scipy) and place the n_grid final points where the fitted curve passes through equally spaced fractions of its rise (8%…92%). Each grid point then buys a distinct point on the energy/reward frontier instead of another sample of the plateau. If the fit is poor (R² < 0.5) the grid falls back to log-uniform over the bracketed active region. The refined points are evaluated as well, so the output is the whole measured curve, not just a proposal.
  4. Report for the target N. MPC's transition is approximately N-invariant (λ is per-step normalised through dt, and the N=4…10 study showed the same knee), so the grid transfers. HardFlow's floor λ ≥ N·lr/2 is not — it is 5 at N=4 and 12.5 at N=10 — so the grid is re-cut at the target N's floor and any dropped points are named.

Outputs per run: autorange.json (everything), evaluations.csv (one row per λ evaluated), curves.png, and grids.txt with ready-to-paste --sweep_lam lines. Stopping rules, tolerances, budget and the proxy configuration are all flags.

04Recommended grids vs. the suite grid

05Does a grid found at N=4 hold at the target N?

The ImageReward MPC grid from the N=4 search was run as-is at N=10 (H=2, n_inner=12, jump surrogate, same seed), plus one plateau point at λ=2. The uncontrolled image differs between the two N (r_unc −1.44 vs −1.60), yet the transition sits at the same λ: Δr crosses 0.5 at λ≈0.19 and reaches ≈2.9 by λ≈0.35 in both. Five of the six grid points land on the rising part of the N=10 curve; the lowest (0.072–0.08) is inactive at both N and is the one point a tighter budget would drop. This is the property the whole approach rests on — λ is per-step normalised through dt in MPC, so the knee is a function of (reward, prompt), not of N — and it held.

06Using it for an unattended sweep

Two scripts, both in fmocc_core/. Neither is committed yet.

lambda_autorange.py does the range finding and writes the grid:

CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0 python lambda_autorange.py --reward imagereward \
    --prompt "a portrait of a man" --edit_prompt "a smiling face" \
    --methods mpc hardflow fmrg --probe_N 4 --target_N 10 --n_grid 6 \
    --flux_device cuda:0 --reward_device cuda:0 --out_dir results/autorange/imagereward
# -> grids.txt:
#    mpc      --sweep_lam 0.072 0.13 0.19 0.25 0.36 0.65
#    # hardflow: saturated_at_admissible_floor -- every admissible lambda (>= 12.5 at N=10) is on the plateau ...
#    hardflow --sweep_lam 50 79 130 200 320 500
#    fmrg     --sweep_lam 0.35 0.77 1.2 1.8 2.8 5.5

auto_sweep.sh chains it into the real sweep, one run_sweep.py invocation per method at the target N on exactly the grid found, with --resume so a killed run picks up where it stopped:

TARGET_N=10 MPC_H=2 MPC_NI=12 SEEDS="42 43 44" \
    ./auto_sweep.sh 0 imagereward "a portrait of a man" "a smiling face"
# results/auto/imagereward/<prompt-slug>/{autorange, mpc_seed42, hardflow_seed42, fmrg_seed42, ...}

To leave a batch running over several reward × prompt combinations, loop that line; each combination costs the range finding (≈10–15 min at N=4 for three methods) plus the sweep itself. The card is pinned with CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES and both device flags stay cuda:0 in-process, which is the only safe way to use the second card with ImageReward.

Knobs worth knowing

  • --tol (default 0.05): flatness as a fraction of the reward ceiling. Raise it for noisy rewards; the noise floor measured earlier today was ~0.05 in J for ImageReward MPC at low λ and considerably larger for HardFlow.
  • --max_evals (10) and --n_grid (6): bracketing budget per method and the number of final points. The refined points are evaluated too (--evaluate_grid false skips that and roughly halves the cost).
  • --endpoint_surrogate jump is the default for MPC range finding because it is ~3× cheaper and its transition matched the rollout's within noise at N=8; the sweep itself still runs whatever surrogate you give run_sweep.py.
  • --hardflow_lr_inner: HardFlow's admissible floor is N·lr/2. If the finder reports saturated_at_admissible_floor, this is the knob — the transition is below the floor, and lowering lr is the only way to reach it.

07Caveats

  • One prompt pair, one seed. The grids above are for "a portrait of a man" → "a smiling face" at seed 42. The point of the tool is that it re-derives the grid per combination; the numbers here are a demonstration, not a table to copy.
  • The ceiling from REWARD_OBSERVED_RANGE is the model's, not the prompt's. CLIP's documented maximum of 0.80 gave a ceiling of 0.65 here, while the attainable plateau on this prompt pair was 0.24. The first version of the stopping rule waited for the ceiling and walked λ up to 3.5×10⁶; the current rule judges flatness on the observed rise and stops within a decade of the plateau. The ceiling is still used as a sanity bound and for the tolerance scale.
  • Saturation is not the only failure mode at high λ. FMRG on CLIP shows Δr falling above λ≈3500 while energy grows past 10⁹ — the image is being destroyed and the reward model scores the wreckage lower. The "falling" stop exists for exactly this; with only the ceiling test, those runs looked like "not saturated yet".
  • The prior is a decade-level heuristic. λ₀ = c·Δr_max/‖∇r‖ landed within about a decade of the knee on both rewards and both were used to set c, so it is calibrated on n=2. A wrong prior costs a few extra bracketing evaluations, not correctness; the bracket does not depend on it.
  • Proxy N=4 changes the uncontrolled image (r_unc differs from the target N), so the curves' absolute Δr differ between N. What transfers is the λ location of the transition — section 05 measures how well.
  • FMRG's response is a cliff, and it is not reproducible near it. On ImageReward it goes from Δr 0.09 at λ=0.77 to 3.15 at λ=1.2; the first pass measured 2.94 at λ=0.83 and the second 0.09 at λ=0.77 — the trajectory bifurcates. An open-loop gain has no energy term pulling it back, so a small change in λ (or in kernel nondeterminism) flips which basin the sample lands in. Grids for FMRG should be denser across its knee than the logistic quantiles alone give, or averaged over seeds.
  • Above its floor, HardFlow is not regularised in any useful sense here. At λ=50, N=4 its penalty weight is reg = N/(2λ) = 0.04. On PickScore it pushes Δr to 9.6 — past MPC's plateau of 5.0 and past the reward's documented maximum — at energy 400 vs MPC's 16: it is reward-hacking the scorer with a destroyed image. The floor λ ≥ N·lr/2 is what keeps it out of the regime where the comparison would be fair; lowering --hardflow_lr_inner is the fix, and the finder now says so explicitly when it detects the plateau at the floor.
  • HardFlow's run-to-run nondeterminism (documented earlier today: three identical commands spread Δr by ~70% of the smallest value on CLIP) makes its curve the least reliable of the three at a single evaluation per λ. Its grids should be read as the right decade, not the right point.
